At US Letter, 12 pt Courier and about 43 lines per page, the plugin estimates about 100.4 pages; the PDF's 101 pages include the title page |

**Why it holds up against the *Screenplay* standard:**

- No scene numbers: no production numbering (`1.`, `2.`) in the body.
- Almost no transitions; the few `CUT TO` / `FADE` lines are handled as action.
- The title page carries only the title and the writer (the `FINAL DRAFT` banner from the PDF was dropped).
- Master scenes only: action + character + parenthetical + dialogue, with people built out of what they do and say.
- 101 pages, close to the book's hundred-page target.

**Small exceptions**: the opening and closing keep `we slowly zoom in`, `close-up` and similar camera language; the book says to avoid it and keep only the rare unavoidable case. Here they are handled as action, with no new element type invented.

**Why Sintel is no longer a library case**: it is a final polish / production draft with scene numbers, shot directions and `CUT TO`. It is useful for watching action density and visual storytelling, but it is a poor template for spec-script format. It stays in the open-script list below for anyone who wants to read a production draft.

## Open scripts you may legally reuse

- **Blender Studio open movies**: Sintel, Elephants Dream, Big Buck Bunny, Cosmos Laundromat, Spring, Coffee Run, Charge, Wing It! and others. The films and production material are released under Creative Commons Attribution; the project pages often include the script, beat sheet and step outline under **Script / Production files**. Most are production drafts (scene numbers, camera notes) — good for visual storytelling and action density; strip the production marks before using them as a format model.
- **Scripts of public-domain films**: *Night of the Living Dead*, *His Girl Friday*, *Charade*, *Detour* and others. Careful: a film entering the public domain does **not** put its literary screenplay in the public domain; check the script's own copyright status before using it.

## What these examples teach about format

1. **The scene heading is the spine of the page**: interior/exterior, place and time must read in one line.
2. **Action paragraphs break on visual beats**, not on sentences; animation especially depends on what can be drawn.
3. **Keep dialogue blocks short**: character, parenthetical and line form three clear levels of indentation so the reader can scan.
4. **Montage, flashback and inserts** can be handled as action beats inside a scene; not every shot needs its own scene.
5. **A spec draft needs no production scene numbers**; numbering arrives with prep. Leave it to the scene rail.
6. **Tell spec drafts from production drafts**: scene numbers, camera angles and `CUT TO` mark a production/shooting draft. Read unmarked spec drafts first, then produced drafts, so production detail never gets mistaken for writing convention.
